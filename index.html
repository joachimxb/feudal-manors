ACKS II’s nobility stops at the 160-family baron. Beneath it sits the layer feudal armies were actually made of — the knight’s fee. This module prices that layer from the game’s own economics: what a fee earns, what it owes, and what its service is worth.

Two RAW facts carry all the math on this page: domains are priced in gp per peasant family per month, and a sworn man's wage doubles per character level (25 / 50 / 100 / 200 / 400, RR p.168). Know those two and every number here will reconcile.

The claim in full — the doctrine stated, with its citations

The claim. A fee is the smallest estate whose income pays its holder's wage (RR p.168) — an operational definition for this tool, not a historical one. Historically a fee was a unit of assessment before it was an estate, and only sometimes both: the crown counted servitia, and what lay under a count was the lord's business. This module needs a number it can price, so it takes the smallest estate that pays its man and prices that — which is a modelling choice, and is the reason every era on this page re-counts the same ladder. Everything on this page follows from that one sentence. Because a sworn man's wage doubles per level, the rungs fall straight out of the henchman wage table — ½ / 1 / 2 / 4 fees, retaining L2 / L3 / L4 / L5 — each netting land + 3 gp per family, each owing as many lances as it counts fees, and the ladder stops where barony income begins. Nothing here is a new economy; it is ACKS II's own economy read at one level below the baron.

What the definition does not model. One estate, one charter, one lord — that is the shape the whole engine assumes, and three real arrangements fall outside it. One fee spread across several manors, so that no single estate answers to the paper. One manor carrying several fees held of different lords, each with its own count and its own definition of record. And fractions split among coheirs — tenths and fortieths held by heiresses, wards and burgesses, which the record shows from the mid-13th century on. All three are ordinary history, and none of them is here: modelling them means giving up the one-estate-one-paper assumption the ladder, the muster and the writ are all built on. The module's answer is the honest one — it says so, and prices the case it can. The era table (History chapter, Library) says the same thing about the divorce of fee from knight.

The one structural change. RAW puts a monthly garrison line on every domain — 2/3/4 gp per family by class, the cost of keeping the land defended. Here the fee is quit of that line by service — quit in the period's own sense: discharged of a due, owing service instead of coin, the quittance being the written receipt that says so. The consideration is a lance — the knight himself, mounted and mailed, with his fostered squire and remounts — and a trained levy, both held to written terms (the assize of the fee), with the host service bounded at one month of service a year in kind and extension at the lord's wages. That single swap is what turns a RAW domain into a feudal one; every consequence priced on this page descends from it.

The terms

Twelve words carry the module. Plain definitions here — the doctrine behind each lives in the Library.

fee — land granted to keep a fighting man: the smallest estate whose income pays his wage.
lance — one knight’s service: the man himself, horsed and mailed, squire and remounts behind him.
quota — how many lances an estate owes: as many as it counts fees.
quittance — the deal itself: the fee is excused its garrison bill because it renders service instead of coin.
the assize of the fee10 — the written terms: mail per fee held, a levy trained and kitted, a view of arms twice a year.
scutage — coin sent in place of a body, when no body can ride.
the levy — the manor’s own yeomen, up to two in ten families, standing the watch.

assessment (current vs ancient) — whether the paper is re-surveyed as income grows, or frozen at the grant.
distraint6 — seizing a man’s goods until he performs what he owes — up to and including knighthood itself.
make-good — the coin that books an old-standard body as the equal of a current one.
the month of service12 — the year’s one month in kind, served at the fee’s own cost (history’s forty days); past it, the lord pays wages.

What this does not model

This is a subinfeudation and service module, not a domain manager and not a manorial economy. Naming the edges is part of being auditable — a reader should not have to discover the boundary by finding a dial that isn't there.

Not the ACKS domain turn. Tax and morale decisions, population growth and migration, stronghold building, market and urban income, harvest variation, casualties reducing the family base, travel and campaign distance, seasonal service, long-run loyalty drift. RAW handles these; the module holds them still. Not the manor's economy. Demesne against tenant land, free against customary tenure, money rent against produce against labour services, mills, courts and monopolies, meadow and common rights, crop systems, hired labour, market access. A family here is ACKS's family — one revenue number — and every one of those distinctions is folded into it. Not a reconstruction of one place or century. The era presets are composites and say so at the dial; the marks above are how you tell which parts are ACKS, which are borrowed, and which the module invented to join them.

And every figure says per what. 108 gp/mo is a standing rate; 211 gp once is capital paid at the grant and never again; 12 gp for the term falls due when the host is called, and not otherwise. Three different kinds of money — and the module’s hardest question, a levy’s 211 gp once against a bachelor’s fee’s 8 gp/mo of surplus, is only legible once they are told apart.

12 · the month of service, and why it needs no free duty RAW's favor⇄duty economy prices relations whose consideration passes repeatedly — the sworn henchman's one free ongoing duty (RR p.345), the outsider's one duty per favor (p.347). The fee is neither. ⚖ Deeper — RAW’s hex caps, the class ladder, and the century
RAW caps a 6-mile hex — ~31 sq mi, ~20,000 acres — at 185 / 375 / 780 families for outlands / borderlands / civilized (RR p.340, Limits of Growth). Density turns a fee's families into ground, and ground is what a lord actually parcels out of his hexes. RAW states the limit per 6-mile hex of a domain's territory — a cap on secured land, while land outside any domain carries no one — so realms interleave settled and empty hexes and the figure is never a national average. RAW even advances the classes at these same numbers (185 → borderlands, 375 → civilized, given a road to a small town within 24 miles and a morale of +1 — or by territory instead, 5 hexes and 925 families, 7 and 1,200; or by founding an urban settlement near a friendly city, which asks no family count at all, p.340). The ladder is a ratchet: downward the cap is absolute — a domain “cannot exceed” it — while upward the advance, once made, is permanent “even if the requirements for the classification are no longer being met,” so thin ground under a high class is ordinary and the reverse is illegal. The dial is RAW's own ladder, and it doubles as a reading of the century: the default sits at the 13th-century heartland (the distraint era — History chapter, Library), Domesday England reads ~120, and a manor itself walks the dial as it stocks and fragments (the three counts of a fee, History chapter, Library). The parceling check below reads the dial in those terms — how many ~1,060-acre manor-territories the hex holds, and how full each has filled.

⚖ Deeper — the assize’s terms, and raising vs. buying protection
The quittance is conditional, and its conditions are written. An assize is a royal ordinance that sets required equipment and inspects for it; this one is written into the fee. Its terms: the count of lances owed (the quota — the charter's servitium, on every card), mail per fee held (Assize of Arms 1181 — a knight owes as many hauberks, full mail shirts, as he holds fees; the sub-knightly man arms at the habergeon, the lighter short mail, the tier the assize set for its 10-mark freeman — a commoner assessed at ~£6⅔ in goods), the levy trained & kitted (the p.341 credit's own wording: "trained and equipped"), and a view of arms twice yearly (Statute of Winchester 1285) — held by the lord's officer under the inspecting-troops delegation (RR p.352–3; the Offices section’s ghost-soldier flag is this view, corrupted). Historically both instruments were national law enforced by royal machinery; writing their content into the fee's charter is this module's deliberate transplant (owned in the ⚖ table). And the assize is era-bound — it is the definition of record's enforcement arm (the definition lever, below): before 1181 there is nothing to keep, kit being custom; 1181 defines and 1285 enforces — the assize written, then the view that polices it; and under the ordinance the fee is commuted and the view survives as the muster commissioner's art. A fee in default is quit for the lance only: it hires the levy's watch until it re-arms.

What the switch actually chooses is not whether the assize stands — the requirement is identical either way — but whether the manor raises its protection or buys it. Kept, the men are its own: a one-time kit bill per man (light foot 88.5 gp, heavy foot 122, the serjeant's 578-gp mounting — RR p.431's Training and Equipment table; note RAW's own worked example on p.432 prices the same two foot at 94.5 and 124, contradicting its table, and this module follows the table, which is internally consistent: 0.5 + 3 + 85 and 1 + 6 + 115), paid once for each man's place in the levy and not again, since his arms and skills pass to his heir (p.432) and the martial tradition carries the place forward. Thereafter their wage-value stands against the garrison line every month, called out or not (p.341), and the mounted serjeant discharges half a lance of the quota besides. In default, the identical protection is bought at market instead: troops at mercenary wage, month after month, for as long as the manor wants a watch at all — and none of it credits toward the lances, which must still be found in bodies or coin. Raising is capital; hiring is rent, and the rent does not stop.
Fosterage (squiring) — a squire rides with the lance HÂRN

Adds each knight’s fostered squire — +45 gp of watch-and-train value at zero land cost, where a boy actually rides. Every lance keeps an attendant; fosterage decides whether it is a boy of the class or a hired groom. A realm’s custom, not a century’s.
⚖ Deeper — fosterage: the heir’s education as the lance’s train
Knights place their sons in other knights' households at ~14 — typically up the ladder, in the hall of the father's own lord, whose court is the clearinghouse — "fosterage" borrows the Celtic name for what English custom called squiring; attested up the aristocracy, extended here to the whole class as a norm. The heir earns his levels before he inherits, and every lance gains a squire (~45 gp of medium-horse wage-value, RR p.169) at zero land cost — the boy's wage is his education. He keeps his knight horsed and stands the manor's watch, but takes no place in the line of battle. Reciprocal, so it nets out realm-wide. The boy is not a henchman and takes no slot — fosterage is a placement between households, not a retainer's contract, and the contract that carries his knight disowns the slot machinery outright (⚖ the bundle is closed). Every knight in the field keeps an attendant regardless: three or four horses and sixty pounds of mail are not a one-man job. What fosterage decides is which kind — a boy of the class, who costs nothing and can stand the manor's watch, or a hired groom, who costs coin and cannot. So the limit is supply, not permission: all knights may squire, and there are not enough boys to go round — the class carries well under one boy of squiring age per lance (the supply of boys, Design chapter, Library), so a realm at canon's paper rate of one squire per lance is describing its assessment, not its muster. A practice, not an entitlement: the toggle sets the realm's custom, but at any given hour not every lance has a boy in it — heirs come of age, fosterings lapse between sons — and the Judge should feel free to leave particular manors empty. The +45 rides only where a squire actually does.

⚖ Deeper — surveys, escheat, inquest, and the margin
A fee's count is written at the grant and polices kit, not wealth: the view of arms inspects mail against the charter, and no one re-rates a manor for prospering. The paper is rewritten at escheat and re-grant, at new enfeoffment, or on the record of a royal inquest (Domesday, the cartae of 1166, Kirkby's Quest — the inquiry finds the facts and the new paper follows it; generational, resisted, dear). Current assumes the survey is fresh: assessments track income, the module's default behavior. Ancient freezes the counts at the charter while incomes grow past them — the standing-manor line (land lever, above) then shows the margin, the income the paper never sees. The margin is the household's funding (Muster section), distraint is the crown taxing it by income instead of re-surveying, and a realm where the margin is universal has aged into livery (the era table, History chapter, Library). And the count is not all the paper writes — the definition of the lance rides the same charter (the definition lever, below).

⚖ Deeper — kit inflation, the covered horse, and the definition lag
The charter writes three things: the count of lances, the definition of a lance, and the term — and all three reset only when the paper does (the assessment lever, above). The definition drifted by statute: the 1181 Assize of Arms wrote the first kit ladder into law (mail per fee held), and Winchester 1285 enforced it — not a new price but an enforcement overlay: wealth-assessed kit and the twice-yearly view of arms policing the same body the 1181 assize defined. The field standard tightened separately: by the 13th century royal cavalry summonses begin specifying equus coopertus, the covered horse, precisely because men were arriving paper-compliant and obsolete. Kit inflation ran ahead of old charters, old fees under-delivered by current standards, and lords preferred coin and modern hires — the definition lag is one quiet engine of commutation in this model; history ran several at once (fractional fees, campaigns past the forty days, overseas service, the crown's own taste for paid, flexible hosts — Keefe's caution against reading scutage as a single story). This dial re-prices the requirement: fee-counts, quotas, and the discharge menu all follow it. And because a charter keeps its own definition, paper and muster can disagree: the grown manor (Muster section) carries a charter-definition dial, and the lag is computed there, not narrated. Distraint — the seizure of a man's goods until he performs what he owes — is the crown's definition-reset aimed at the man rather than the manor: distraint of knighthood compels the income-qualified to take up current-standard arms and dubbing (defined in full on the Library’s History chapter).

Why service is 1 and tax is 0 — one derivation, and it can be proved wrong
Not a pile of arguments. One residual allocation inside a calibrated model, and it is stated this way so that a Judge who disagrees knows exactly which number to attack.

At land value 6 the customary profile is calibrated to a net receipt of 4 gp per full-tenement unit — that is the historical input, drawn from the manorial record (see the evidence in the Library). The ledger then closes itself: 4 = 6 + service + tax − 3, so service + tax = 1. At ACKS's whole-gold-piece resolution there is exactly one point of non-land recurring revenue left to place, and the only question is where it sits.

RAW answers that: service revenue is the licence income of skilled freemen — "Most of these services are provided by freemen who offer a portion of their labor to the ruler in exchange for the right to practise their trade on his domain" (RR p.341) — and a customary manor still has mills, smiths, bakehouses and brewing. So the residual is not zero, and it belongs on the service line. Tax takes what remains, which is nothing.

Tax 0 is not a claim that the dues vanished. Heriot, merchet, entry fines, court profits, tolls and assized rent were all real and all collected. It says this profile resolves no separate whole-gp monthly tax line beyond a total recurring receipt that is already calibrated. Note the trap in a weaker version of this argument, which this page used to make: every individual due being smaller than RAW's smallest digit does not make their sum smaller than it. The honest claim is about what is separately resolved, not about what is negligible.

What is booked where. Agricultural week-work is represented through the land line, because that labour produces the demesne output RAW's land line already collects — so labour-rent is not priced twice. That is a bookkeeping convention, and its limits are worth stating: customary tenants commonly owed works and a fixed money rent on the same holding. The thirteenth-century Extenta Manerii asks its surveyors for "how much rent of assize be paid yearly besides the works and customs" — so cash rent and labour service were two obligations, not one. This module does not resolve them separately; it does not claim they were the same.

Relief, aid and wardship are a different layer entirely — they run from the fee-holder up to his liege and replace recurring tribute at that level. They neither duplicate nor excuse peasant dues below it.

How to falsify this. Move the calibration target and the allocation moves with it. If rural service ought to round to nothing, then tax is 1 and service 0. If local cash dues demonstrably need another whole gold piece, then either the target net is too low or this decomposition is wrong. Every step is a number you can argue with.

Tax 0 is in any case RAW's own dial at its floor (p.349), and it pays the domain +2 morale for sitting there — the manorial bargain stated in RAW's own table: the lord takes less in coin and is owed more in obedience. The lord's own line follows: where he hires the garrison instead of receiving it in service he nets feeNet − 2/3/4 by district, so at the customary rent an outlands demesne nets exactly nothing — the frontier held in hand pays for its own protection and no more.
The scutage rate — what coin buys off a lance MODULE
What does it cost to commute one missing lance?
What a fee pays when no body rides. RAW anchor: the estate's own Call-to-Arms value, 1 gp a family — the default, and RAW exactly. Customary Angevin, John's and later negotiated price the century's postures, from about a quarter of a substitute's hire down to a decayed sixth. The requirement in full: the crown charges the whole recorded requirement, and commutation stops being a bargain — though under the ordinance even that buys no lance, the paper standing at 100 and the lance costing 145.
⚖ Deeper — why the cheap rate is the interesting one
Scutage is not priced at what it replaces, and that gap is the whole institution. English scutage ran about 20s per fee against a knight's 2s a day for forty days — roughly £4, so about a quarter of a substitute's hire. The crown took the coin and still had to find the man; the difference came from fines for respite, aids at the three occasions, wardships, and reliefs — which is why a king's military finance is never one line in a ledger, and why barons fought over the rate rather than the principle. Set the dial to the requirement in full and the argument disappears wherever the paper and the hire agree: no lord would ever prefer coin, commutation dies, and the fee stays a body forever. Under the ordinance it does not disappear — the requirement is still the Angevin 100 while the lance it must replace costs 145, so rendering the paper in full leaves the crown 45 gp short of a man. That residue is the ordinance's whole bargain. Set it to the RAW anchor and coin is so cheap that a Judge should expect his knights to reach for it — which is exactly what the record shows them doing. Keefe's caution applies: scutage was never one story, and the rate moved by reign, by campaign, and by who was arguing. The dial does not model that drift; it lets a Judge pick a century's posture and hold it.

Manors at a glance

Cost to lord = income he'd net keeping the land in demesne — the land a lord keeps and runs himself; with the garrison hired for cash that is the fee-holder's net less 2 / 3 / 4 per family at civilized / borderlands / outlands (the c/b/o triplets used throughout — the district popover prints the sum at your current dials). Lord's net = retainer-equivalent − cost. Read it as a comparison and never as coin: what the same men would cost as standing hires, set against the income he gave up to have them instead. The cards split it into the three ledgers it was hiding — a garrison bill avoided monthly, a body owed once a year, and a wage not being paid — because those are three different kinds of number and only the first is a saving that recurs. The surplus of enfeoffment is largely the garrison bill he no longer pays, which is why knights profit a lord most on the frontier — and why the militia table below, where he pays that bill in yeomen instead, is the comparison that actually decides it. Each rung ≈ 2× the one below (wages double per level), so fees count in powers of two in the grade they retain: 1 fee keeps an L3, 2 an L4, 4 an L5 — what the fee grows unaided is one level shy (the knight's contract, ⚖ below).

Reading the manors — recruits, wage-grades, XP plateaus, and the two wage ladders

Recruits is market logic — but it is consulted only at escheat: line extinct, fee forfeit, or new land enfeoffed. In the steady state the fee is heritable and reproduces its own holder — the heir squires out at ~14, inherits at L1–L2, and grows into his grade. Primogeniture's landless younger sons are, in turn, exactly the pool of market knights the recruitment logic prices. Wage-grade is the henchman rate the fee must cover to keep him once he holds it. Raises to is XP logic — RAW pays rulers campaign XP each month from domain income above a level-based threshold (p.423), so land literally levels its holder over the years — and the dubbing decides the ledger. A dubbed knight is on the knight's contract (⚖ table below): his expected wage comes off domain income before the XP threshold applies (RR p.342 + Major Errata r4), so the fee grows him to one level below what it can retain — the last level is won in the field, never behind a plough. The undubbed esquire deducts nothing (p.423) and is the one man land alone completes. Knights are made by war; the fee only keeps them.

And that is an incentive to refuse the belt — say so at the table rather than let it be found. On the ledger alone, staying an esquire is worth a level: the undubbed man deducts nothing, so land completes him where it stops his dubbed neighbour one grade short. If nothing stands against that, a player has been handed an arbitrage and the module has said nothing about it. What stands against it is not arithmetic and is not in these cards — it is distraint of knighthood (the crown compels the belt and fines the refusal; the Crown-follow-up selector in the Muster is that writ), exclusion from the offices and the commissions that only a knight holds, a worse hand in every marriage and wardship negotiation, and the plain fact that a lord chooses which of his men to seat on the better fee. The ledger advantage is real and it is meant to be; it is priced in standing, and standing is the Judge's to charge.

Granting land away can raise Personal Authority, and that is an abstraction talking, not a strategy. RAW measures PA against the domain a ruler personally administers, so enfeoffing land off the demesne can improve the number while the realm is unchanged. Three readings, and the module holds the third: (1) a legitimate consequence of RAW's abstraction — a smaller personal domain genuinely is easier to hold in one man's hand; (2) a module exploit, if a ruler shuffles land purely to move a statistic; (3) an administratively costly restructuring — enfeoffment is heritable, hard to reverse, creates a man with claims of his own, and Quia Emptores 1290 exists because rulers legislated against exactly this practice. Read it as the third. A lord who grants to game his PA has bought the number with land he cannot get back.

Two wage ladders meet here — know which is which. The mercenary table (60 gp regular / 100 gp veteran heavy horse) prices a soldier on the open market: kit, remounts, and no tenure. The henchman table (25 / 50 / 100 / 200 / 400) prices the stipend of a sworn man, whose loot-share and heritable tenure are the rest of his pay. They cross at 100 gp — a dubbed knight's market floor (the F1 veteran) equals an L3 stipend — which is exactly why one 12-family rung serves knights of L1–L3, and why the esquire's fee keeps an L2 veteran on the half-fee’s 63 gp at ACKS RAW revenues — 60 at the customary rent — who would cost 100 as a hired lance: the tenure is the discount.

What breaks the bank

The finding, plainly: at the wage-parity floor no rung of the knight's ladder keeps a standing fighting man on wages — structurally so: a fresh floor's rounding surplus is always less than one family's output, at most 11 gp under any land value, and can never meet even the 25-gp L1 wage. The quota above one lance is affordable only in coin or for the term, and that is the record's own answer (scutage, the term sergeant). The standing mesnie lives in two places only: the margin — the grown manor whose ancient assessment never caught up, which is why distraint hunts exactly those men — and the barony, where RAW's henchman roster simply resumes. Between the floor and the margin stands the household every knight does keep: the domestic establishment inside his threshold, below.

The two cost regimes — keep in peace, wages in war

A retained man is never priced as a standing mercenary, and the mistake of doing so is what makes households look impossible. In peace he is kept, not hired: the fighting household man is a henchman at his own wage (25/50/100 by level) — part of it rendered as the hall's board in kind (~16 gp/mo of the supply rate, man and mount) — and the bond, not the board, is the discount: a 25-gp L1 henchman stands where a hired sergeant costs 60. Below him the valet draws no wage at all — he is the domestic establishment, inside the threshold (next section): his wage is his place, as the squire's is his education. (The fifteenth-century paybooks kept the same two lines: the ordinance lance paid its coustillier — a fighting rider, this ladder's man-at-arms — and carried its page unpaid; the Library’s History chapter’s lance note has the whole arc.) In war the split follows RAW's own favor-and-duty law (p.345–6) — two RAW halves and one transplant. RAW's halves: a Call to Arms is provider-pays — the called vassal musters at his own wage cost, so the fee’s month of service rides on the knight's purse (priced on every Ladder card: about two-fifths of the fee's income, once a year); and the Troops favor runs the other way — what the lord sends, the lord pays. The transplant (owned in the ⚖ table, Library): RAW bounds duties by count — one per favor, no free service — never by days; the module reads the land as the standing favor that earns one month of service a year, and prices anything beyond it as new consideration at the muster-roll wage — the lord's coin. So the estate carries the term and nothing past it: the keep is why a hall could hold men a wage-ledger says it cannot; the lord's wage past the forty days is why a thin fee could stay in the field beyond them at all. One boundary note: host supply is the lord's line throughout (Units section), so a term-hire's bill on the fee is the wage alone — the ledger shows both the month-of-service sum and the monthly reserve it implies.

The household inside the threshold

Before any wage is paid, every knight already keeps a household — inside his cost of living. RAW prices a level's expected living at its own wage line ("living expenses… equal to the wages of a henchman of the same level" — 25 / 50 / 100 / 200 / 400 gp/mo, p.173), and counts what that spending buys: servants, stabling, an implicit rent of the hall itself (p.173). The XP threshold above it (25 / 75 / 150 / 300 / 600 gp/mo, p.423) is a different ladder — the income a man of each level should command with ease, half again the spending from L2 up — so the establishment fits inside the threshold with headroom. That spending is the domestic establishment — grooms, a porter, a huntsman, men who take up a bill when the manor is entered. So the bank-breaking table above is not "knights had no households"; it is the sharper claim that matches the record: servants who could fight were universal; fighting men on wages were magnate practice. The armed valet lives on the living-expense line, below the threshold; the household knight lives in the margin.

The warded walls — castle-guard

The castellany's arithmetic looks like a paradox — a castle-holder who cannot pay watchmen — until the period's own answer is priced: the castle is warded by other men's service, not the castellan's purse. Castle-guard was a render like any other: fees bound to a castle owed ward in rotation (the honour of Wallingford's fees, History chapter, Library), and serjeanties — small tenures held by the service of a man at the walls — clustered at castles because the castle-holder is the one lord land-rich enough to carve them. This is the quittance doctrine pointed at the walls: the ward is discharged in kind by tenure, and the garrison line the castellan would pay is quit by the same instrument that quits his own. Enfeoffing is the land-rich lord's move; retaining is the land-poor knight's — a bachelor never carves seven families out of his twelve.

At the barony, RAW resumes

The quota is a doctrine of the knight's ladder only — ACKS RAW gives barons no fee-count; their duties run by favors, tribute, and the Call to Arms as written. And the baron needs no household rules either, because RAW already has them: the mesnie is the henchman roster — 4 + CHA sworn men at the wage table's rates, the 9th-level magnate adding companions who take no slot (p.334–6). How full the hall runs is a matter of demesne: at average land a 160-family barony clears ~320 gp/mo above the L6 wage when civilized, ~160 at borderlands, and nothing at outlands — the district selector above prices it, and note what does not move: a fee's own net is class-free (land+3 under ACKS RAW revenues, land−2 customary) — the quittance's quiet gift, and it is the class-freeness rather than the figure that is the point: it is why marcher realms ran on fees while their barons' demesnes ran thin. Where the surplus exists, the binding constraint is never coin but Charisma: the sworn household ends at the cap, and past it stand non-henchman retainers at base −2 — annuitants, liveried men, the bought and re-bought. The affinity begins where Charisma runs out.

Worship — why the surplus becomes men

"Worship" is the period's word for standing, and it worked like a credit rating. In a world with no police, no bureaucracy at the village, and royal courts that could not enforce their own judgments, protection and justice were private goods, produced by networks of lordship — your rights were worth exactly the following that would turn out for you. From that one fact the whole economy of lordship follows. Coin that stays coin cannot defend itself: the rich man with no following is the one whose manor is entered, whose suit is lost to a packed jury, whose heiress is married away — prey. Coin converted into men is security, litigation-power, marriage-market weight, and the only ladder toward lordship — which is why the moral literature praised largesse and damned avarice: it was encoding a political truth. So above the quota's floor, the margin does not accumulate; it recruits. A lord offers good lordship — protection, advancement, offices, his weight at law — and receives service, counsel, arms, and presence; followers wear his badge so that all men know who would come if they were crossed; and the fatal thing said of any lord is that he could not keep his men.

In the module's terms worship is not a new mechanic — it is why the existing ones point the way they do. The loyalty ladder (Design chapter, Library) is the asset being purchased: household henchmen at 0, companions at +2, the affinity's annuitants held near 0 only by continuous good lordship — service must be continuously bought, one favor per duty, and the household's keep is that purchase made monthly. Arbitration is its exercise: most disputes ended not in a verdict but in a loveday — a settlement imposed by a respected lord under the implicit weight of both followings — and being asked to arbitrate is itself worship, the castellan's unofficial office (his official ones are the Offices section’s). And the corruption dial has its mirror here: the unsworn officer converts the crown's revenue into his own level; the lord converts his own revenue into men — one is graft, the other lordship, and both are the same arithmetic of income made into power.

The assessment — when the paper is written

The margin that funds all of this has a legal engine, and the module dials it (the assessment lever, Dials panel). A charter writes three things — the count of lances, the definition of a lance, and the term — once, at the grant ("holds by the service of N knights," priced from the land's income by this module's own table), and the assize then polices kit against the paper, never wealth: no one re-rates a manor for prospering. The paper is rewritten at exactly four events: escheat and re-grant (the new charter prices at current income — the same event where recruitment logic runs, Ladder section); new enfeoffment, including subinfeudation; royal inquest (Domesday, the cartae of 1166, Kirkby's Quest — generational, resisted, dear); and distraint, the crown's cheap workaround — which rewrites nothing and instead taxes income directly (the £20/£40 lines), forcing the dubbing and with it the knight's ledger, converting the holder's margin from household money into the crown's service. Under current assessments the survey is fresh and the module behaves as it always has. Under ancient, counts — and the definition with them — freeze while incomes grow, and the margin opens — 107 gp/mo on a 23-family manor still charted at one fee, at average land: two household knights, or a hall of men-at-arms. Distraint is the crown noticing; the era where the margin is universal is bastard feudalism (the recovered layer, History chapter, Library) — frozen quotas, grown incomes, and every margin wearing livery. One honest cross-current: the frozen count capped the crown's scutage basis too, which is why crowns abandoned the fee for the income tax; and a holder might seek re-assessment upward — banner eligibility, a marriage, worship — the social pressure against pocketing the margin quietly forever.

RAW's channel for the vassal's person is Call to Council, unpriced (p.346) This is the module's structural deviation — owned, bounded, priced (the p.342 aside about "spending only the minimum 2gp" reads the line as payable, not zeroable, and we zero it). The form is a quittance, the period's own instrument: charters made estates quit of geld (the land tax) and of castle-guard in consideration of service kept, and quit-rent is the same deal run backward — coin paid to be quit of service. The fee exercises the scutage entry's equivalence standing: troops instead of gp, permanently. Because the discharge is contractual, no valuation of the knight is ever needed — asked who actually defends the manor, the answer is the consideration itself: one knight, his squire, and his armed yeomen (in the yard, the knight is still the garrison — the quittance is how the ledger says it). The quittance is conditional — see the assize row below: a fee in default is quit for the lance only and hires its missing watch. The bound against strict RAW is small: full militia credit already equals the RAW fee-holder's net land+3 at civilized; the residue is the frontier increment, 1–2 gp/family — and it expires: the fee's own maturation walks it to the civilized reading (the three counts of a fee, History chapter, Library), amortizing the concession over the manor's life. Equivalently, at realm scale: land held by knight-service carries no garrison line — enfeoffed families garrisoned in men, demesne families in coin; the per-fee quittance and the class exemption are one fact in two ledgers.
The assize of the fee — the levy is a term of enfeoffment: mail per fee held, yeomen trained & kitted, view of arms twice yearly; a fee in default hires the levy's watch until re-armed RAW militia is optional — a ruler may levy up to 2 per 10 families (p.432) — but the garrison credit's own wording sets the standard: "trained and equipped" (p.341–2), with kit heritable (p.432). The officer delegations include inspecting troops (p.352–3) The Assize of Arms (1181) owed a hauberk per fee held — mail scaling with fee-count in statute — with its 10-mark freeman at the habergeon tier (his was a chattels assessment — movable goods, not land: the half-fee mapping borrows the kit-grade, not the tenure); the Statute of Winchester (1285) added the twice-yearly view of arms. Both were national legislation on all free men, enforced by royal machinery — juries and itinerant justices in 1181, elected hundred-constables in 1285. Writing their content into the charter of the fee, and their enforcement into the lord's household, is this module's deliberate transplant. Hârn's yeomen (double tenancy, own kit) are the same custom in canon. Age works against the assize, too: the mail-grade yeomen are precisely the whole-holding tenants, and fragmentation thins that stratum (the three counts of a fee, History chapter, Library) — the old subdivided manor is where the view finds habergeons pawned and the fee slides toward default. The convergence is a calibration, not a law: at land 6 — RAW's own average — the rungs' heavy-foot ceilings count 1 / 2 / 4 mail-grade yeomen on the 1-, 2-, and 4-fee rungs, the statute's scaling out of RAW's qualifying numbers, the esquire at the habergeon tier (0); richer or poorer hexes shift the counts. Default is priced, not forbidden: −levy-credit gp/mo of hired watch until the one-time re-arming is paid — distraint (the seizure of goods to compel a defaulter) as texture, on RAW numbers. And so is delivery: when the service is called, the knight pays his men's wages himself (provider-pays — Call to Arms, p.345–6); the month-of-service bill is on every card, and it cuts into the fee's net for the term — one month of service a year; past the forty days the lord's wages carry the host.
The quota — a fee owes as many lances as it counts fees: ½ owes joint service, 1 its holder, 2 and 4 the holder and the count beside him. A lance is one knight-service, and discharge is by body: a heavy horseman serves a lance whole, a medium-horse serjeant at two-for-one, light horse and foot not at all, coin at the scutage rate — the assize's mounted serjeants credit ½ each while it is kept. And the definition of a lance is itself the charter's, drifting by era (the definition lever): the Survey's mailed rider (60 gp — the fees re-count in him), the 1181 knight (mail per fee), the statute knight of 1285 (Winchester's enforcement overlay on the same body; the covered-horse summonses pressing past it), and the ordinance lance that absorbed the crew — the fee by then commuted. Priced on every card and in full on the Muster section RAW's Call to Arms prices the duty in gp — troops worth 1 gp/family (p.346) — and never counts lances; the wage ladder alone would read "4 fees = one L5 knight" and stop there The servitium debitum counted knights — one fee, one knight's service; two fees, two — and the Assize of Arms scaled mail with fee-count: the second hauberk of a two-fee man was a second man-at-arms's mail, not a better coat for the first. The module keeps both truths: the fee retains a grade (the wage ladder) and owes a count (the quota) — the gap between them is where scutage, the term-hire market, and the household live. The discharge is priced, not assumed: at wage-parity floors the count above one is affordable only in coin or for the term, which is the record's own answer — real multi-fee holders scutaged and hired; standing retinues awaited the margin (Muster section). The 2:1 substitution is the servitium's own record — two serjeants accepted for a knight (Pollock & Maitland carry the cases) — but the pinning is the module's calibration, owned here: the historical serjeant was a social and tenurial category, not an equipment tier. We map him to the ACKS medium-horse body, and rule the 60-gp heavy-cavalry man sufficiently equipped to discharge a service whole, as the pay rolls' esquire-grade men-at-arms did.
The knight's contract — dubbing puts the fee-holder on the henchman XP ledger (expected wage deducted before the threshold) and raises his base loyalty from −2 to 0 for as long as the fee's service is kept — the same conditions the quittance already audits: homage at succession, the assize, the render. There is no separate bond to track: the loyalty rides the fee. A fee in default of its service is a bond in question — −2 until it re-arms, and still deducted. The esquire's fee sits outside the contract: RAW's plain vassal, undeducted, base −2 RAW keys the wage deduction to henchman status: "henchmen vassals subtract their expected monthly wage from domain income for purposes of calculating XP" (p.342, Major Errata r4 form); a non-henchman vassal deducts nothing and holds base loyalty −2 (p.347), −4 beyond the range of trade (p.347; the range itself defined JJ p.203). The ledger: companions "function as henchmen for all purposes… but do not take up henchmen slots" (p.334–6) — the book's own proof that henchman accounting detaches from the slot system and travels; we move one clause of that bundle onto the dubbed fee-holder. The loyalty: senatorial republics give "non-henchmen assigned to rule vassal domains base loyalty scores of 0 instead of −2" because the institution commands the loyalty (p.355) — and tenure by knight-service is feudal monarchy's same institution. The loyalty attaches to the position, not the person, which is why the heiress's husband who renders the service is as sworn as the man born to it — the exact shape of the senate parallel. The clauses ship together because RAW never sells them apart: nowhere does the book pair the wage deduction with negative base loyalty (henchman 0, companion +2) — a deducted man at −2 would kneel to be made strictly worse off, and no one kneels for that. Dubbing is thus a real exchange: the knight's last level moves to the battlefield, his lord's hall moves closer. What falls out downstream — the esquire as the one man land alone completes, and distraint of knighthood emerging from the arithmetic — has its own section on the Library’s History chapter.
The bundle is closed — the fee makes a sworn fee-holder, and he carries two clauses of RAW's henchman machinery and no others. Scope: this defines what the fee does to the man who holds it. A knight without a fee is not a lesser sworn fee-holder — he is RAW's henchman, governed by RAW's henchman rules entire, and this module rules nothing about him. What attaches: the XP ledger — expected wage deducted from domain income before the threshold (p.342 + p.423, errata r4 form) — and base loyalty 0 instead of −2, for as long as the fee's service is kept (p.355). What does not attach: no henchman slot (he does not count against 4 + CHA); no slot-chain prerequisite, including p.168's ≥ 50%-of-wage licence to hold henchmen of his own; no wage entitlement (the fee is his pay, p.168); no hireling morale check (the quittance's conditions are the audit — homage at succession, the assize, the render); not the henchman duty economy (p.345's one free ongoing duty at a time, running until revoked — the fee's standing service rests on its own rule, the next row, not on henchman machinery); and not the inner circle's termless service — the mesnie's true henchmen ride without term, the sworn fee-holder is term-bound. A clause not named here does not attach, and may not be invoked elsewhere in this module to settle a question about him. RAW has no partial status. It has henchmen, companions and plain vassals, each a complete package, and it never sells a package by the clause — except once, and that exception is this module's whole warrant: companions "function as henchmen for all purposes… but do not take up henchmen slots" (p.334–6). RAW is silent on what a man carries when he is given some henchman accounting and no henchman slot, because RAW never puts anyone in that position Because a status defined only by what it adds will keep growing, and this one did — twice, silently, in opposite directions. The contract row above announces "two transplanted clauses." By the time this ruling was written the module was using four — and the count was not the tell, because the count was right. Both extra clauses were borrowed at the point of use, and both were borrowed wrongly. The duty economy (p.345 against p.347) was doing the heaviest work in the module from the Service booked at the knight's full wage row — grounding the fee's entire standing service — and had never been examined, because it had never been declared; declared, it fails, being henchman status machinery that describes a true henchman vassal rather than a fee-holder (the next row grounds that service in tenure instead). And p.168's ≥ 50%-of-wage licence had been transplanted into the fosterage lever to decide which knights may take a boy — slot machinery, and applied backwards at that, since fosterings run up the ladder into the great halls the rule excludes. That is the argument for closure in one line: a clause nobody has to declare is a clause nobody ever checks. Neither of these was caught by being wrong. They were caught by being written down. The contract's own warrant is the companion clause, whose entire content is "…but do not take up henchmen slots" — a transplant that disowns the slot system cannot inherit the slot system's prerequisites. So the negative list is normative rather than illustrative, and the closure rule is the operative sentence: to add a clause, add it here, with its precedent and its price. Borrowing one at the point of use is how the module came to hold two incompatible readings of the same man at once, and how a load-bearing transplant came to sit outside its own definition. This is the doctrinal form of the rule the code already lives under — one rule, one owner — and it is checkable the same way: a normative citation of henchman status machinery outside these rows is a finding. RAW's henchman wage table (p.168's 25 / 50 / 100 / 200 / 400, and the landed-vassal clause that offsets a wage against domain income) is a price list, cited freely and correctly throughout; only the status clauses are closed here.
The fee's service stands because the fee stands — a tenure held of the lord in perpetuity, whose consideration passed once and in land at enfeoffment, reserves its service as an incident of the tenure, not as a favour that must be renewed. It is bounded by the term — one month of service a year, history's forty days — and it ends only by the process that ends tenures, distraint and then resumption, never at the lord's word. Scope: this rules the standing service of a fee and nothing else — not the man, not any other obligation he may owe, not any unlanded service. A knight without a fee owes what ACKS says he owes RAW's favor⇄duty economy prices ongoing relations between parties whose consideration passes repeatedly — wages, gifts, grants renewed and revoked (p.345 for the sworn henchman, p.347 for everyone else). It has no category for a conveyance with a reserved service, because ACKS assigns land to a holder and then governs him through loyalty and wages rather than through tenure. RAW is silent here, not contrary — and the distinction it lacks is the one this module is about Because the alternative was to keep borrowing p.345, and p.345 is henchman status machinery that the contract row expressly disowns: a transplant that gives up the slot system cannot keep drawing on the slot system's economy — and the fee's single most load-bearing property was resting on exactly that. What it rules over: the standing character of a fee's service, and only that. The lord does not re-purchase the host's attendance each year; he purchased it at enfeoffment, and goes on purchasing it every day he does not resume the land. It is not a better deal than the clause it replaces — it is a different deal, and in two ways a worse one for the lord. RAW's henchman duty runs until revoked, at the lord's word; the fee's service cannot be revoked at all, only distrained and then resumed, by process. And where the henchman's free duty is open-ended in kind, the fee's is bounded to the term. The fee trades the lord's flexibility for the tenant's permanence, which is what tenure is. And it explains the scutage, which p.345 never could: if service were a revocable favour, a lord wanting coin instead of a lance would simply revoke it, and no commutation instrument would be needed. It is because the service is an incident of a standing tenure — not the lord's to cancel — that commuting it requires its own machinery. The module already has that machinery; this ruling is what makes it necessary rather than merely available.
Knights owe service, not tribute — no per-fee tribute line; tribute falls once, on the lord's whole realm RAW tribute cascades to every vassal by realm families (18 × fam0.6, p.343–4) — but the formula was calibrated for baronies and up: on a 12-family fee at ACKS RAW revenues it would take ~80 of 108 gp (74%) — and at the customary rent it is worse, not better: 25 families owe 124 gp against an income of 100, which is confiscatory, because RAW keys tribute to family COUNT and lowering the rate does not move the formula. Tribute is explicitly negotiable ("Adventurers can set the tribute for their own vassals as desired," p.343) Below the barony the render is the lance itself — a ruling inherited from Knightly Realms. The Call-to-Arms duty (troops worth 1 gp/family, p.346) is what cash-vassalage would extract; the fee delivers a 100-gp lance instead.
Service in kind side-steps Tribute Inefficiency Cash tribute decays with vassal count (p.347): 9–16 vassals deliver 66%, 17–63 deliver 50%, 64–216 a third, 16,384+ just 1% (the Drakomir example). Only ≤8 vassals pay in full. One boundary is RAW's own: the printed table lists 16,384 twice — in "16,384 or more" (1%) and in "4,096 – 16,384" (5%). This module resolves the overlap to the stricter 1%; every other boundary is inclusive and matches the printed rows exactly Service never passes through a collector's hands — the knight delivers himself. And the 100% band (≤8) coincides with the henchman cap (4+CHA): RAW's own economics wants the feudal shape — a handful of sworn tribute-vassals, and below them men who pay in person. A knight-heavy realm is poor in coin and rich in lances by design.
The sworn officer cures Tribute Inefficiency at a lawful 12.5% — the Offices section’s fisc oversight The inefficiency bands are unconditional: the loss goes to "crime, corruption, or waste," and nothing in RAW says appointing a steward recovers it. RAW's only officer machinery is the four expense-line magistrates at 12.5% (p.342) — none collects revenue Owned plainly: this buys out a designed scaling limiter, and it is the quietest big change in the module. Can a ruler really count his own person against garrison cost? Said plainly: this is the module's structural break with RAW — one line, bounded, priced. Strict RAW puts a garrison line on every domain and treats it as a floor to be paid (its own aside pictures a ruler "spending only the minimum 2gp," p.342). This module rules the fee quit of that line so long as its service is kept: the lance and the trained levy are the consideration, on the model of charters that made estates quit of geld or castle-guard for service kept, and on RAW's own printed, realm-conditional equivalence — "in some realms, a vassal may send troops instead of gp" (p.346; this module's realms declared such) — exercised standing. The size of the break: at civilized, full militia credit zeroes the line by pure RAW; the entire concession is the frontier increment, ~1–2 gp per family — and it is time-bounded as well as small: a fee walks outlands → civilized as it stocks and fragments (the three counts of a fee, History chapter, Library), so the concession is largest at the grant on a raw march and amortizes to zero as the manor matures — a founder's subsidy that expires, not a standing tax. What the quittance form buys is not a smaller break but a cleaner one — the discharge is contractual, so the knight is never priced onto the line and no gp valuation of the man is ever needed; and it has teeth: a fee in default of the assize is quit for the lance only and hires its missing watch until it re-arms. Asked who actually defends the manor: one knight, his squire, and his armed yeomen (the full ruling: ⚖ doctrine table, Library).
The magistrate keeps 12.5% of a line he oversees; the quit fee books the whole line as income — isn't that minting XP? RAW already mints campaign XP from the garrison line for a military man: the captain of the guard's salary is carved from garrison expenditure, "does not decrease the funds available to pay his troops," and "grants campaign XP" (p.342) — precedent for the kind of move, not the amount. Against the RAW-legal baseline — militia credit maxed — the quittance books 0 extra XP-bearing income at civilized, ~11 gp at borderlands, ~23 at outlands (the 12-family fee at the ~2.1-gp credit ceiling, below), beside the captain's lawful 4.5–6 gp on those same lines: between 0× and 4×, and less than RAW at civilized. Nor are the two equally secured: the captain's eighth is free money — troops unreduced, nothing extra delivered — while the fee's income books only while the consideration is delivered: a veteran lance and a trained levy actually standing there, on terms the view of arms audits twice a year. Per gp of XP, one buys the realm a garrison, the other an accountant. And a 12.5% share presupposes a domain with a staff; at fee scale the org chart collapses — captain, garrison, and ruler are one man with one purse. You can't pay a man an eighth of himself.
Is XP double-counted — does the knights' campaign XP subtract from their lord's? No gold piece appears on two XP ledgers. The knight XPs on his own domain income minus expected wage (p.342 — the knight's contract extends this to every dubbed holder, ⚖ table); the lord books nothing from the fee — no tribute, no coin; and the service render, like RAW's own scutage, "does not count as domain income for XP purposes" (p.346). A service realm concentrates less XP up top than a tribute realm — the coin and its XP stay on the land, in the knights. That distribution is the Arthurian texture, chosen on purpose.
Doesn't the knight piggyback on his lord's Personal Authority? No rule shares PA (Personal Authority — RAW's measure of whether a ruler's level is big enough for what he holds; 0 = matched, negative = the domain outgrows the man) — it is computed per ruler, per domain. And the fees sit on the PA-competence diagonal by construction: the PA table's income columns are the GP-threshold bands (verified cell-by-cell against p.348), so each rung's wage-grade reads exactly PA 0 over its own manor — the book's own Designer's Note says rulers advance to PA 0 and stop. Under the knight's contract the land delivers a man one level shy of that mark: every dubbed knight reads PA −1 until the field finishes him, and PA 0 is the badge of the proven knight — the module's texture as law, not a leak. The esquire, outside the contract, ploughs to his grade and reads PA 0 by rents alone. Nothing is borrowed, because nothing is missing.
Doesn't the in-kind garrison inflate knightly income? Run the full RAW ledger (revenue land+4+2, less the three 1-gp cost lines, less garrison, plus militia credit — p.341–2) at RAW's own militia ceiling — 2 per 10 families at the qualifying numbers' best mix, half heavy foot and half bowmen, ~2.1 gp of credit per family, good even uncalled (richer mixes exist on paper, but the yeoman-and-archer mix is the period's) — and it yields land+3 exactly at civilized class. The entire dispute is the frontier increment, ~0.9 gp per family at borderlands and ~1.9 at outlands — call it 1–2; concede it whole and the bachelor's fee moves from 12 families to 13 at borderlands (ACKS RAW revenues; at the customary rent, 25 to 34). What the gold per family buys is a sworn knight in the manor house above the armed peasantry — and p.432 (rebels are drawn from the militia when a domain turns) is exactly why the assize pairs the levy with a resident lord: the counterweight is a term of the same contract.
The enfeoffed knight is no household henchman — why does the wage table bind him? Because the wage table is the market price of a level-L man, and the fee must pay it at every point of contact. At a fee's birth from the household, p.168 applies verbatim: the lord enfeoffs a knight he was already paying, and land replaces wage. At a birth from the market — RAW lets a lord enfeoff non-henchmen outright: "mercenary commanders, adventurers" (p.347) — the fee's income is the recruitment offer, and it must meet the man's open-market wage or he rides on. And at every inheritance the market binds the grade, not the man: an heir on a 96-gp manor doesn't ride off — he stays, poorer, and the estate can no longer sustain the rank's establishment, so the line slides down the kit ladder toward the esquire (the threadbare fee, Design chapter, Library) while standing manors re-grade rather than resize. Netting, offering, retaining: one price — enforced hard at the first two points, and at the third by the slow slide of grade.
Who pays when the levy is called up? Someone does: the provider — RAW's general law (Call to Arms: the vassal musters troops at his own wage cost; Troops favor: the lord pays for what he sends down, p.346). The rung cards price it: afield, the knight pays his yeomen's wages and their plough-teams stand idle. The idle plough is booked at the family's full revenue rate (land+6 under ACKS RAW revenues, land+1 customary — the engine reads the live profile), because RAW's two ledger columns come apart here: p.432 reduces domain revenues by one family per man levied ("produce revenue as if it has [fewer] families"), while p.342's Costs per Family column — liturgies, tithe, maintenance — keeps charging: the family still lives there; only its production marches. Net loss per man is therefore the whole revenue, not the fee's net take. Together roughly two-fifths of the fee's income for the month of service, plus −2 morale — free to keep, dear to call, which is why historical levies were short-term, defensive instruments. And the bill is bounded: the fee owes the term — one month of service a year, the forty days — at its own cost; past it the muster rides on the lord's wages (the pay ladder, 1s/2s/4s ↔ 60/100/200), as the rolls show fee-holders drawing. The knight himself is pre-paid for the term: the fee sustains him year-round — at home always, in the host for the forty days, and beyond them at the lord's coin.
Four fees — one L5 knight, or four knights? Both, and the module says which is which. The wage ladder answers retention: 400 gp of income keeps one L5 man — grade. The quota answers service: four fees owe four lances — count. Historically the count was the law and the grade the social fact, and they were never traded one for the other: a four-fee holder did not send "one better knight" — he sent himself and found three more, or paid. The cards price the finding (scutage, term-hire, the levy's horse), and the Muster section prices why the standing alternative — retained men on wages — belongs to the margin and the barony, not the parity floor. Concentration keeps its real cost visible too: four L3 lances out-rate one L5 on the battle sheet (Units section) — the premium of the great fee is command, quality, and one sworn bond, never bodies.

Played strictly by RAW, a sub-baronial vassal pays his lord in coin, and the arithmetic leaves a low-level man on a big manor — out of the box, ACKS builds a classical realm. This module's structural change is one: the fee renders service instead of coin and is quit of the garrison line for it, at a cost of about one gold piece per family against strict accounting. Around it stand a set of explicit rulings — no per-fee tribute and its inefficiency side-step, service booked at full wage, the assize, the quota with its definition of record, the knight's contract, the squire, heroic-unit pricing, the household's keep and the assessment (Muster section), and the fisc oversight (its corruption dial lives in the Offices section) — each quoted against RAW in the ⚖ doctrine tables (both in the Library). Count them all and the tally is twelve rulings, not one: the difference is that the rest are extensions where RAW prices nothing, not overrides of what it does.

The structural cause — one yield where there were two estates

A manor had two economies under one lord. RAW has one, and it faces the ruler. Demesne production, tenant rent, labour services, monopoly dues (mill, oven, fold) and taxation are collapsed into a single ruler-facing per-family yield. One number is asked to be a rent, a wage-equivalent, a profit and a tax at once — which is why it cannot be calibrated against any of them individually. HISTORY

Four consequences follow, every one visible in the English record and invisible in RAW: peasants accumulate (a good decade leaves the tenant richer, because his obligation did not move — hence the peasant land market, entry fines, dowries and wills); the lord's income does not track prosperity (grain prices roughly doubled 1180–1300 and assized rents did not move, which is why lords turned to direct demesne farming, tallage and entry fines); risk changes hands (a bad year in ACKS is the peasant's problem, on a manor it is the lord's, and manorial court rolls are largely a register of arrears); and custom is a real constraint — legal power over villeins was near-absolute, practical power was not.

And the fifth is why this belongs in this module. The remedy is the incident, not the rate. The peasant's assized rent is to his lord exactly what the lord's charter is to the crown: a fixed paper that cannot be revised, with income growing away from it, reached only by event — entry fine, tallage and heriot below; relief, aid and wardship above. The module's own thesis, one level down. Run a manorial superstructure on a fiscal substructure and this is the seam.

Custom — what the dial actually rules

The custom of record changes two numbers: a peasant family's service revenue from 4 to 1, and its tax revenue from 2 to 0. Urban families are untouched. Tax 0 — a residual allocation, not a claim that lords took nothing

The derivation, and it is the load-bearing passage of the whole package. MODULE At land value 6 the customary profile is calibrated to a net receipt of 4 gp per full-tenement unit — the historical input, drawn from the manorial record. After the three fixed expenses that leaves exactly one whole gold piece to divide:

4 = 6 + service + tax − 3 ⟹ service + tax = 1

RAW says most service revenue comes from skilled freemen and trade rights, and a manor still receives value from mills and rural trades, so the residual is not zero and belongs on the service line. Tax takes what is left, which is nothing. This is falsifiable three ways, which is the whole reason to prefer it over the four arguments it replaced: move the calibration target and the allocation moves; if rural service ought to round to zero then tax becomes 1; if local cash dues demonstrably require another whole gold piece then the target is too low or the decomposition is wrong. The module's one free number is now the calibration target — an empirical quantity a reader can check against the record — rather than a rate nobody can audit.

The booking convention, and only what it claims. RAW-INFORMED MODULE Agricultural week-work is represented through the land line, because that labour produces the demesne output RAW's land line already collects. So labour-rent is not priced twice. The strong form — "customary rent in general is already contained in land revenue" — is withdrawn: customary tenants commonly owed works and a fixed money rent on the same holding, and the thirteenth-century Extenta Manerii instructs its surveyors to record "how much rent of assize be paid yearly besides the works and customs." The note on resolution — a statement about notation, not a defence of a fudge. RAW's domain economy is written in whole gold pieces per family per month, and its smallest possible entry — 1 gp — is ten shillings a year. An English knight's fee of £20 with about 25 recorded entries yielded its lord sixteen shillings per entry per year, and that sixteen shillings is everything: assized rent, the value of works, court perquisites, merchet, heriot, tolls and the mill. The individual dues inside it run one to four shillings apiece. So tax 0 is the statement that no seigneurial due is large enough to earn a line of its own in a ledger written in whole gold pieces — they are collected into the calibrated total rather than resolved separately.

⚠ Note what that no longer says. It used to conclude that every due "falls below RAW's resolution", which silently promoted each is small into all of them together are small — an aggregation fallacy. Sixteen shillings against a ten-shilling digit leaves plenty of room for the aggregate to reach a whole unit, and part of it does: that is precisely the gold piece the module assigns to services. The claim is about what gets its own line, not about what the dues were worth. The reader's objection — but they did take merchet — is conceded inside the argument, which is what makes it land.

What the incidents do and do not carry

Relief, aid and wardship model extraction upward, from the fee-holder to his liege, and stand in for recurring tribute at that level. They do not replace, duplicate or excuse peasant dues below it. If this module ever wanted incidents to stand literally in place of RAW's smoothed tax line it would have to model the peasant-level incidents — heriots, merchets, entry fines, court receipts — or annualise an equivalent set. It presently does neither and does not claim to.

Design — what this module does, and why MODULE

What this module does

ACKS II's Titles of Nobility stop at the 160-family baron; everything beneath is a single undifferentiated "domain." But the layer beneath the baron is where feudal armies actually came from — the knight's fee. This module prices that layer with RAW economics under one declared doctrine — every ruling flagged in the ⚖ table: the henchman wage table fixes what a man of each level must be paid; a fee is the smallest estate whose income pays him (RR p.168 — a henchman-vassal "does not need to be paid a wage if the domain income is equal to or greater than his wage"); the mercenary tables set the recruitment floor. From those inputs alone fall the rung sizes, the powers-of-two "counting in fees" idiom, the lord's profit from enfeoffment, and the XP plateaus. Where the model must choose between ACKS's classical default — free-floating rulers paying cash tribute — and the feudal pattern, it chooses feudal and says so: every such ruling is quoted against RAW in the ⚖ doctrine table, in the Library. History enters as consistency checks — if the reconstruction is honest, its ratios should match the medieval record without ever being tuned to it; where they land, that is corroboration, not proof.

Lineage

This is the third generation of an idea. The Wandering Gamist's "ACKS Estates – Nobility" (2017) first worked the knight's fee in ACKS terms: a ~10-family estate as the support unit of a lance, with the decisive observation that feudal service is seasonal — which is why land-for-service beats year-round wages. Helgeran's Knightly Realms systematized it into a whole pyramid: uniform 10-family knightholds under 2nd-level knights netting land+1 at RAW revenues (the garrison still paid in cash, centralized as the baron's 852-gp foot), banners of fifteen fees run as micro-senates, and service in place of tribute below the viscount. This module refines toward the historical record: a graduated scale of manors (esquire to castellan) sized by wage-parity instead of uniform knightholds; the garrison taken in kind (the knight's own lance and trained yeomen in place of the coin line); heritable fees with fosterage demographics closing the XP loop; fidelity through the household rather than the senate; and the cross-checks (History chapter, Library). It states the doctrine as a quittance — the fee is quit of the garrison line so long as its service is kept, the period's own instrument (estates quit of geld or castle-guard for service; quit-rent is the same deal run backward) — and writes the conditions down as the assize of the fee: mail per fee held, levy trained and kitted, view of arms twice yearly, with default priced on every card.

The engine — levels first, history second

Be clear about the causality: these manors are generated by ACKS character levels, not by history. The engine is the henchman wage table (RR p.168), which doubles per level — 25 / 50 / 100 / 200 / 400 gp — and one farming fact: a fee-holder who garrisons his own land in kind keeps his whole net per family. The rungs below are worked at ACKS RAW revenues — land + 3, so 9 at average land — because that is the ledger this derivation was written against; under the module's opening customary rent the same wages buy 15, 25, 50 and 100 families instead. Divide wage by net and the rungs fall out: ~7, 12, 23, 45 families. (That 23 misses 2 × 12 by one on purpose: grants come in whole families, so each rung rounds its own remainder up — two bachelor's fees carry two rounding pads where one double fee carries one. The same arithmetic prices two esquire's fees at 14 families against the bachelor's 12: consolidation wastes less remainder, one more reason lords preferred it — and real fee assessments never doubled cleanly either. One honest cross-current: with fosterage on, every lance brings its own squire, so many small fees carry more squire-value per family than one great fee. But fees are territories, not permanent family-counts — two 12-family fees are two manor-territories that will each stock toward ~23 and re-grade (the parceling check below), so the lord's real choice is how many territories to parcel, and per territory the mature state is the greater fee.) Richer land shrinks these floors, as it should — fees were income assessments, and the hide (the old English tax-assessment unit of land, notionally 120 acres) was itself physically smaller in fertile shires; but a standing manor never resizes, it re-grades: the same estate assesses at more fees and keeps a better knight (see the lever's standing-manor line). Because the wage doubles per level, each rung is twice the one below — which is why the medieval idiom of "holding N fees" maps directly onto levels — the grade each fee retains, its proven holder: ½ fee keeps an L2-grade man, 1 fee an L3, 2 fees an L4, 4 fees an L5 — and onto counts: the same fee-number is the quota of lances the estate owes (the ⚖ quota row; the discharge priced on every card). Grade and count part company above one fee — the wage keeps one better man while the law owes several — and that gap is where scutage, term-hire, and the household live (Muster section). One scope note: these counts are the distraint-century definition's — the definition lever re-counts them per era (a Domesday "fee" is the 60-gp rider's) — while the wage ladder beneath never moves. (What the fee grows unaided is one level shy of that — the knight's contract; the idiom names the man the fee is sized to keep, not the boy it raises. And the later age's fractional fees — tenths and fortieths — are the divorce of fee from knight that the era table dates; the ladder models the marriage, not the divorce.) The manors stop at L5 because the next doubling (800 gp) is a barony's income (160 families × 7 = 1,120 gp, plateauing at L6 against the 1,200 XP threshold): a level-6 man is a lord, not a knight. History enters as the check — if the reconstruction is honest, its ratios should land inside the medieval record's own ranges. The cross-checks (History chapter, Library) show where they hit exactly and where they sit at the edge of the band. A fee is, throughout, a job posting with a fixed salary: its income must match what the man's sword earns on the open market, or he rides elsewhere.

Making sense for the lord — the other half of the schema

A rung only exists if the lord profits by granting it — otherwise no fee is ever enfeoffed and the manors are a fantasy. The ledger closes from his side too, and it is two-colored: demesne families garrisoned in coin, enfeoffed families in men — demesne × the garrison rate in cash, plus one lance per fee, and no garrison line under the fees at all. That is the servitium debitum's — the "service owed," a realm's fixed knight-quota — own arithmetic: a quota of knights, never a commuted garrison budget. His cost is the demesne income foregone (land+1 / land / land−1 per family at ACKS RAW revenues, land−4 / land−5 / land−6 customary — in his hands the garrison is paid in cash); the service he receives is the holder's market rate (plus the fosterage squire) — and the fee's two readings agree by construction: N whole fees owe N 100-gp lances, and the anchor is N × 100 (only the half-fee rides above its 50, at history's 60), so the retainer-equivalent is the same figure whether the card counts one graded holder or the quota's lances — and under other definitions of record the identity holds at their own body-price, exactly so under the Survey's 60. At wage-parity fees his net is families × the garrison rate (2/3/4), less the grant's small rounding pad (3–8 gp): the surplus of enfeoffment is the garrison bill he no longer pays. That is why the deal is stable from both sides — the man can get no better wage elsewhere, and the lord turns a profit at every rung, deepest on the frontier where garrisons cost most. It is also why frontier lords historically seeded knights along the marches.

Three more load-bearing integration points. Tribute: assessed once, in cash, on the lord's whole realm (18 × families0.6, RR p.343–4); knights owe service, not coin, so there is no per-fee tribute line — the same lance garrisons the fee and answers the lord's call. This is doctrine (see the ⚖ rulings table, Library), but RAW rewards it twice over: the tribute formula was never calibrated below ~100 families (on a 12-family fee it would confiscate 74% of income), and cash tribute decays with vassal count — Tribute Inefficiency (RR p.347) hands the collectors a third of every payment at 9–16 vassals, half at 17–63, and 99% in the Drakomir example's 21,600 — while service in kind never passes through a collector's hands. Only ≤8 vassals pay in full, and that band coincides with the henchman cap: RAW's own economics wants the feudal shape — a handful of sworn tribute-vassals, and below them, men who pay in person. Density: by c.1300 England ran ~1 knight's fee per ~170 recorded entries — about 68 families at the 2.5 convention, which against a customary fee of 25 families says the crown, church and demesne held roughly two thirds un-enfeoffed (dubbed knights were rarer still) — the drivers were demesne retention, the fragmentation of fees, and the fact that a fee's land fed many more households than the one that named it; many fees were held by scutage-paying esquires, widows, and wards — the demesne share — what fraction of a realm its lord keeps in hand rather than enfeoffing — is exactly that dial. This page does not model it: every rung here prices a fee that is enfeoffed and is manned, one at a time. A realm's knight-density is the Judge's own decision, and the historical band is roughly a third to a half of the land enfeoffed, the rest held by crown, church, and demesne. The levy: ACKS's militia rules (2 per 10 families, qualifying numbers, kit and skills passed to heirs — RR p.432) are the Assize of Arms of 1181 in game form — and at land 6 the rungs' heavy-foot yeomen even count 0/1/2/4 on the ½/1/2/4-fee rungs, an echo of the statute's hauberk-per-fee scaling (a resonance, not a cross-check — the statute armed the knight, not his ploughmen; see the cross-checks table). The module writes this into the assize of the fee on every card — the levy as a term of enfeoffment, audited by the twice-yearly view of arms (Winchester 1285) that the Offices section’s inspection delegation already staffs — and prices default: a fee that lets its levy lapse is quit for the lance only, hiring its watch until it re-arms. And the qualifying numbers enforce the deepest structural rule: the first levied heavy horseman needs a 60-family pool, so the levy cannot make a knight — knights are made by land, footmen by levy.

Scutage — the quittance run backward

The fee's standing deal is a coin-obligation discharged by service; scutage is the same equivalence exercised the other way, for the term — the year’s month of service discharged in coin (both directions are printed in the one entry, RR p.346). In the two-colored ledger the fee changes color for one war: its render arrives as coin, and the lord hires a replacement into the host. The exchange always trades down — the lawful floor is the Call-to-Arms quota (1 gp/family, p.346), the market replacement is a 60-gp heavy horseman, and what the host lost was a 100–400 gp wage-grade veteran — which is why lords prefer the men, and why the rates ratcheted for two centuries. Two things scutage never buys out: the assize (readiness is a condition of the tenure, not of a campaign — the view of arms runs regardless), and the XP ledger ("scutage does not count as domain income for XP purposes," p.346 — the coin as XP-inert as the service it replaces).

Two instruments wear the one word, and this module prices both. RAW's scutage (p.346) is a tax on a tribute vassal: 1 gp per family in his realm, payable every month until the duty is revoked, imposable more than once, and — the clause that governs everything below — it counts as garrison expense for the vassal who pays it. The fee's scutage is a different animal with the same name: a commutation of a standing tenurial service, the year's month of service discharged in coin. The realm is taxed; the fee commutes. Both are printed in the one entry, which is why they are so easily run together — and the realm's monthly form is priced where it belongs, on the vassals card of Feudal Realms, not here.

Why the fee's render is quantized to the year's month, and why that is not mere smoothing. RAW's monthly rate never travels alone: it is netted against the 2 gp/family garrison line every domain owes (p.341), which is what makes a standing monthly charge bearable at all. A fee has no such line left. It was made quit of its garrison at enfeoffment, by the service itself — the quittance, argued in the ⚖ table on Feudal Domains — so the offset RAW pairs with the monthly rate has already been spent. Carry the monthly rate onto a fee anyway and four things break at once. The fee pays twice for one obligation, in service year-round and in coin every month. The coin/service equivalence collapses, and with it the quota ledger, which rests on a lance and its commutation being the same debt settled two ways. The rate lever inverts: at twelve times the annual figure the RAW anchor would overtop even the requirement in full, so full replacement — the setting that is supposed to be the dear one, the counterfactual where nobody commutes — would become the cheapest on the dial, and the lever would argue the opposite of the history it was built to model. And it would price out scutage's own clientele: the wardships, widows and infirm of the next paragraph are precisely the fees that cannot field a body, and a monthly charge would swallow the minority's whole income — turning the quittance's lawful patch for the incapable into the fastest way to lose the land. The division is doctrine and says so; the quantization is what the quittance leaves behind.

Who pays it is the elegant part: the scutage-payer's lance stays home, so the natural clientele is history's own — wardships, widows, dotards, the infirm: fees that cannot currently deliver a man. That is the quittance's lawful patch for the minority — the wardship pays coin until the heir can ride, the tenure never lapses, and the manor's watch is meanwhile the best-kept in the realm — its levy and household never leave the yard (the Household muster prices this literally: set the assize serjeants to remain, and the whole obligation settles in coin with no body riding — scutage buys out deployment, never readiness). The household inner circle rides, for their bond is the service (priced, at last, in the Muster section); scutage is the instrument of the enfeoffed mass and the incapable. Commutation also has a geography the model predicts: the quittance amortizes as manors mature (the three counts of a fee, History chapter, Library), so scutage takes hold first in the old, dense core while the young march fees keep real service — and the record agrees: scutage clustered in the settled heartland, and the marches kept genuine knight-service longest. And at realm scale it is the era table's dial: one commuted campaign is a footnote, but a realm where scutage becomes the norm has slid from the distraint century into the indenture century (the era table, History chapter, Library) — service melting into coin, on schedule. The module does not forbid the slide; it prices it and names the destination. Scutage works here as what it was: the escape valve whose overuse is the end of the system.

What the fee buys that coin cannot

An army in being, not an army in waiting. In RAW an army is not decided into existence — it is recruited: each realm holds a finite crop of every mercenary type, surfaced only by paid searching and replenished on the scale of seasons (RR p.428). The crops are small — a barony's crop contains no heavy horseman at all, and even a kingdom's 680 heavy horse take three seasons to muster — and the Judges Journal's recruitment vagaries can double the price or empty the market outright. Coin stores value, not readiness. The fee is the period's own solution: it plants a warrior tradition that maintains itself — the knight sits armed, mounted, and obliged on his own ground; his heir squires out and comes home trained; his yeomen pass kit and skills to their sons (RR p.432). A lord of enfeoffed fees rides the week the beacon lights, at the strength the manors price; his cash-rich neighbor is still paying finding fees to learn what troops exist.

And the realm it builds feels heroic — on purpose. Run a realm's military surplus through fees instead of wage-troops and its host fills with leveled men: banners of L1–L5 knights fight as heroic units among the companies of foot (RR p.466), worth several times their weight in hired horse. In peacetime those same men are out and about — errantry, tournament, escort, feud — because a fee obliges a man, not a garrison post. This is true to the period, and it is a dial on tone: an ACKS campaign run on knightly manors drifts toward Arthurian epic — named riders on every field, and adventure quartered in every manor.

Fidelity — whose man is he?

RAW grades a lord's men by their bond, and every loyalty score takes the lord's CHA on top — p.166 applies Charisma to all loyalty scores alike; it is no henchman perk. The henchman vassal (base 0) is the household officer — but a lord holds at most 4 + CHA henchmen, so the inner circle is small: the banneret, the castellan, the marshal. At 9th level with a stronghold a magnate attracts companions (RR p.334–6): L1–3 men who "function as henchmen for all purposes" including vassalage, occupy no henchman slot, and start at +2 loyalty — the crown's and the earls' instrument for seating household knights on fees. The dubbed fee-holder stands at base 0 by the knight's contract (⚖ table, Ladder section): the loyalty rides the fee and its service — hold by knight-service and keep the assize, and the base is 0 whoever inherited — exactly as RAW's senatorial republics hold their vassal-rulers at 0 instead of −2: the institution, not the person, commands the loyalty (p.355). Outside it — the esquire, and the fee in default of its service — stands the plain non-henchman vassal (RR p.347): base loyalty −2, and −4 if his manor lies beyond the range of trade of the lord's largest town (the range itself: JJ p.203 — the trade maps double as loyalty maps). His fee's service still stands — that rides the tenure, not the bond (⚖ the fee's service stands because the fee stands) — but nothing else does: beyond the reserved service he is p.347's non-henchman at one duty per favor, none free, so everything the lord wants above the charter must be continuously bought with good lordship — gifts, offices, monopolies, marriages. The term divides the tiers, too: the household rides without limit — that bond is the oath, not the fee (its price: the Muster section) — while the enfeoffed mass owes the year's month of service, then wages. These "penalties" are the medieval texture, not a defect: enfeoffed fidelity was contractual, not devotional — homage, distraint, and forfeiture all exist because enfeoffed men defaulted. And a pillaged fee is an unpaid knight (going without wages is a calamity → loyalty roll), so frontier defeats cascade into treason exactly as they should.

Fosterage is where loyalty above the floor is made. The knight's contract is deliberately impersonal: base 0 comes with the fee and its service, whoever holds it and however he came to it. The tiers above that floor — household henchman, companion — are personal bonds, and fosterage is the manufactory: the heir squired out at ~14 — up the ladder, into the hall of his father's own lord — comes home a made man of that household, eligible to be taken as henchman or companion. History ran the same direction, and by law where it mattered most: wardship made custody of the minor heir, and of his marriage, an incident of the tenure itself — the orphaned boy was raised in his lord's hall, and the heiress's husband was that hall's choice. Even the nursery of loyalty rode the fee. A realm that stops fostering keeps its base-0 contract knights; what it loses is the next reign's supply of men worth more than the minimum — the fosterage lever is fidelity manufacture as much as XP delivery. And the better bonds cannot scale: the henchman cap holds the hall to 4 + CHA sworn men, so a baron takes his castellans and double-fee knights as henchmen and they hold the bachelors — subinfeudation (RR p.343, the Quintus example) is the only legal shape for a realm of sworn men. Knightly Realms solved the same scarcity the other way: banners of fifteen fees organized as micro-senates (RR p.355), knights voting, base loyalty 0 — equally RAW. Choose by flavor: the household, or the hundred-court.

The supply of boys — and why they pool. MODULE The doctrine table rules that all knights may squire and that the binding constraint is the supply of boys (⚖ squire = watch & train). Here is the arithmetic behind it, with its inputs on the table, because it is a derivation from assumptions and must not borrow the record's authority. In a stationary population the knightly class holds roughly 7 × k ÷ G boys of squiring age per fee: a seven-year window (14–21), a generation length G ≈ 30, and k ≈ 1.4 sons reaching adolescence for each one that inherits — which is what a class carrying ~20% failure of male lines per generation must produce to hold station. That is about one boy for every three fees, and the plausible parameter box runs 0.20–0.56. The third digit is not the point and should not be quoted: every plausible input gives well under one boy per lance, and canon's spear assesses exactly one — paper against muster, arriving from a third independent direction.

And the scarce boys cluster — which the record confirms, with one correction to where. Recorded as a known simplification rather than fixed, because fixing it means modelling household placement — and the mechanism that would do it is wardship, already in this module, since a ward is the lord's charge by law and the law is what moves the boys upward.

Levels drive everything — and the XP loop closes

The rung sizes are retention prices: the fee that matches a level's wage. ACKS's XP rules then close the career loop — and the dubbing decides which ledger his land grows him on. A dubbed knight is on the knight's contract (⚖ table, Library): his expected wage comes off domain income before the XP threshold applies (RR p.342 + Major Errata r4; the wage is "expected" — imputed whether or not coin changes hands), so his fee raises him to one level below what it retains — the 12-family fee pays an L3 but only grows an L2, and the last level must be won in the field. The esquire, undubbed, keeps RAW's plain vassal ledger (income − threshold, p.423) and ploughs to his fee's full grade — the one man on the ladder whom land alone completes, in four or five quiet years. Fosterage is how the field reaches the heir before he inherits either way: squired out at ~14, he comes home L1–L2 and grows into his grade. Domain XP alone is glacial — which is the correct result: knights are made by war; the fee only keeps them. (Why the esquire's exemption is no favor to the knights, and what it produces, is the Library’s History chapter’s distraint section.)

The threadbare fee — below parity

What happens when a fee's income slips under its holder's wage — the manor pillaged, the land value fallen, a line seated above its means? Not a vanishing. RAW already prices the state as a gradient: income below the expected wage is the "without wages" calamity (p.166–7), and a calamity forces a loyalty roll, decaying the score by −1 each time — a slow bleed, not a cliff. The fidelity tier is the tolerance dial: the sworn knight at base 0 absorbs shortfalls that would break a −2 stranger, and the household man rides them out longest — good lordship is measured in the arrears a man will bear. Meanwhile the manor pays part of his rank in kind: RAW's living-expenses list counts an implicit rent of 1/33 of the stronghold's value, the servants, the stabling (p.173) — which is why a threadbare knight lives like a richer man than his ledger shows, and clings to land no wage table justifies. Held long enough, sub-parity resolves the way the retaining rule says: the line slides down the kit ladder — the destrier unaffordable, the mail unrenewed — until the manor keeps an esquire in a knight's hall. That slide is distraint's whole clientele: men on knight's land, below knight's estate, dodging a rank they can no longer carry. The poor knight is not an exception the model tolerates; he is a state it prices.

Source typing — why every count on this page now names its unit

"Recorded household" is retired as a general term. Use recorded entry. A Domesday entry can be a tenant holding, a household head, a smallholding, a dependant, or a servus counted individually. They are not one observation type, and calling them all households smuggles a conversion into the noun — which is exactly how this page came to publish a 5.6× gap that was really 2.25×, by dividing the module's income by ACKS families and the record's by entries.

The convention, and the scope rule that keeps it honest. One ACKS peasant family ≈ 2.5 recorded entries; the honest band is 1.5–3.5. Apply it when converting a source that counts people under a roof. Never apply it to a source that counts holdings. Two repairs Cuxham needed, both visible in the table above. First, the 23-tenant count is 1279 and the ~£40 net profit is chiefly early-14th-century accounting: dividing one by the other is a cross-period ratio and must say so on its face. Second, that £40 is a whole-manor numerator — directly farmed demesne, tenant rents and works, mill and appurtenances, market sales. It can benchmark total estate return; it cannot by itself set the service and tax lines, and the service-1 argument does not lean on it.

The diagnostic, if you are ever unsure whether a conversion has been applied twice: any figure implying more than about 30 acres per recorded entry has had the convention misapplied. 15–25 acres is a correct mixed manor of yardlanders, half-yardlanders and cottars; 47–60 means it was applied to a tenement count that was already right; 120–150 means a tenement count has been divided.

The three counts of a fee — grant, stock, fragment

Ask "how many families is a knight's fee?" and the record returns three numbers — 12, 20, 23. They are one process observed at three moments, and each count has its own derivation. 12 is the grant floor — the least the grant can be: wage-parity (100 gp ÷ 9 gp/family at ACKS RAW revenues; 25 families at the customary rent, and the three counts below are a ratio argument that survives either), the leanest estate that retains its knight. The floor is where the lord's incentive points, since every family enfeoffed is demesne alienated forever — and the record shows grants trending toward it: Domesday's milites on 1.5–2 hides, the Cartae Baronum's "new enfeoffment" fees running smaller than the old, new fees carved from waste and march-land as territory to grow into. A young fee under-fills its assessment — tilling ~360 of the notional 600 acres — exactly as Domesday manors did (Cuxham, above: 15 households in 1086). 20 is the stocked survey: the 5-hide fee divided into whole holdings — 600 geld-acres at the virgate every full peasant household tills, and RAW's own farm agrees: 30 acres per family (JJ p.406; the 20-crop + 10-fallow maths of JJ p.434 is the virgate), so 600 ÷ 30 = 20. A ceiling of whole holdings, not a headcount — the assessor's idealization, exactly as notional as the 120-acre hide itself. 23 is the standing manor: population keeps growing after the slots fill, so the slots themselves split — virgates halve into half-yardlands, cottagers settle the margins with a garden and their labor — and households multiply past the virgater ceiling while each grows poorer. The manor's income rises through it: more rents and court profits from more heads, and above all the demesne worked cheap by the labor the crowding created (Cuxham 1279: 3 free tenants, ~7 half-yardlanders, 13 cottagers — 23 households, double-fee income, rents a mere £5 of the ~£60 gross; the rest was the demesne's corn).

The dial turns itself. Read the last column again: grant → stock → fragment walks the density dial — outlands → borderlands → civilized — on one estate, over two centuries. Commutation becomes endogenous: as the quittance's value fades and the levy pool grows, the old dense core is exactly where service-in-kind stops paying and scutage begins, while young march fees keep real service — and the record agrees: scutage clustered in the settled heartland, and the marches kept genuine knight-service longest. A realm, finally, is a portfolio of stages — grant-stage marches reading outlands, an old core reading civilized — which is what the density lever's "settled-hex, never a national average" caveat means in time as well as space. One honest caveat rides along: RAW's class has a danger face (the garrison rate) as well as a density face (the caps); the arc walks the density face, and the danger face follows only as the march pacifies around the fee — the two usually travel together, since no one stocks a manor under active raids, but a dense fee on a hot border is dense and dangerous. Assarting likewise stretches the footprint and slows the walk. This is a reading of RAW's classes, not a discovery inside them — but what it produces is the module's strongest kind of claim: played forward, domain-seasons deliver the eras in sequence, no dial set by hand — the dynamics reproducing the record's order, not merely its states.

The lance, before and after — a vocabulary note that doubles as a cross-check

The module's lance is the earlier, informal sense: the knight himself, his fostered squire, the remounts. The fifteenth century wrote the word into statute — the Burgundian lance fournie (1468: a man-at-arms, a coustillier, a page; the Ordinance of Abbeville, 1471: those three plus three mounted archers and three foot) — and the paybook, read against this module, corroborates three of its rulings from the far end of the arc. The page rode unpaid and out of the line — the squire-as-train ruling, in a state document. The fighting second rider — the coustillier: brigandine, light lance, the langue-de-bœuf at his belt — was a separately paid combatant, never the boy: the module's split between the fostered squire and the fighting man-at-arms, kept by a quartermaster. And the nine-man lance is recognizably the fee’s month-of-service muster written into a paybook — knight, squire, and trained yeomen re-purchased as man-at-arms, page, archers, and foot once tenure had melted to coin: the era table's slide, visible in a single unit's roster. The missing link is the 1351 French ordinance's gros valet — the armed servant in a habergeon — the threshold's armed servant on his way to becoming a paid line. One rule keeps count and crew straight, there and here: the ordinance counted lances, and the coustillier rode inside one — nine men, one lance. The quota counts the same way: a double fee owes two lances, and built Burgundian-fashion that is two men-at-arms, two coustilliers, two pages, counting as exactly two — the crew multiplies with the count, never against it. Distraint — the word, precisely

The module leans on this word, so define it. Distraint is the period's ordinary legal remedy: the seizure of a man's goods — chattels driven off, profits impounded — to compel performance of an obligation, no court judgment required, only the right. The assize is enforced by it (a defaulted fee is distrained at the view); so are rents, suits, and services of every kind. Distraint of knighthood is that remedy pointed at rank: from 1224 the crown ordered sheriffs to distrain fee-holders, and from 1241 any man of £20/yr in land (later £40), until they took up the arms and dubbing of a knight — compulsion by inconvenience, with respite purchasable by fine (the Fine Rolls carry the receipts). In the module's terms — a reading the module makes, not language any writ used — it is two resets in one: an income instrument that taxes the assessment lag without resurveying the manor, and a definition reset that forces the current standard of knighthood — kit, harness, and ledger — onto the man himself (the definition lever, Dials panel). The record shows the income instrument plainly; the definition reset is the module's synthesis of what the compulsion did. The next section shows why the crown needed it.

The esquire's ledger — distraint of knighthood, out of the arithmetic

This section documents the module's strangest convergence, because it is easy to miss and it is among the strongest evidence the reconstruction has: the rules generate, rather than assume, the central social drama of the thirteenth century. Under the knight's contract (⚖ table, Library), the dubbing is a real exchange. The dubbed man's expected wage comes off his domain income before the XP threshold, so his fee grows him only to one level below its grade — the last level must be won in the field — and in return his base loyalty rises from a stranger's −2 to a sworn man's 0. The esquire signs nothing: RAW's plain vassal ledger applies, nothing is deducted, and his fee completes him — the £24 man ploughs to L2 in four or five quiet years, the only figure on the ladder whom land alone finishes.

Now price the choice as a man on a £20 fee would price it. Take the spurs, and you accept slower growth, a war-debt for your final level, mail and remounts to maintain, and a bond that binds you closer to your lord's call. Refuse them, and your estate finishes you by itself while your obligations stay a plain vassal's. Any rational fee-holder dodges the dubbing — and that is precisely what the record shows: knightly numbers falling through the thirteenth century, men in knight's harness on knight's incomes declining the rank, until the crown began compelling them to take it up (distraint of knighthood — addressed to fee-holders from 1224, to £20 incomes from 1241). In the module's terms, the crown's side of the ledger explains the compulsion: it is not buying the man's prowess — he fights in the same harness either way — it is buying the contract: the sworn base-0 loyalty and the priced, auditable service package that the plain vassal never owes. The record adds two threads the module does not price, and they only deepen the story: the crown happily sold exemption — fines for respite of knighthood were a revenue stream in their own right — and men fled the rank as much from its administrative burdens as from its cost, for juries, the grand assize, coroners' and tax commissions all fell on the county's knights. Refusing an honor because it is priced like an office: the module and the record agree on the behavior.

How much of this was designed in? Less than it looks, but not none. The parts are all RAW's — the wage deduction is the henchman-vassal clause (p.342, in its errata'd form); the loyalty step rests on RAW's own precedent that an institution can hold non-henchman vassals at base 0 (p.355 — written for republics, transplanted here to tenure by knight-service, ⚖ table); the thresholds are the p.423 table — and no distraint rule was written anywhere: men dodge the dubbing because the arithmetic pays them to, and the crown compels because it cannot afford the dodge. Both transplanted clauses were chosen with this history in view, so the claim carries a condition: take these two transplants, each made on RAW-precedent grounds, and the century follows. It also follows by different means than it did in England — XP and loyalty in the model, respite fines and jury burdens in the record — so what comes back is the pressure, not the paperwork. Still, a wage table has no opinion about knighthood, and run forward it returns men refusing an honor and a crown with cause to compel them. The compulsion itself is no mechanic — it is lordship, done with the module's own instruments: dubbing written into the grant, required at succession before homage is taken, or excused for a respite fine. The rest of this chapter checks the model against numbers; this section checks it against behavior — and behavior is the harder test.

The recovered layer — bastard feudalism and the affinity

The politics below the chroniclers' eye-line has a name: since K.B. McFarlane (1945 on) historians call it bastard feudalism DEBATED — lordship exercised through retained men rather than tenure: annuities, livery, and maintenance in place of homage and land. It was recovered late for a concrete reason — high politics survives in chronicles, while the gentry's world survives in court rolls, deeds, and private letters, and only 20th-century county studies (Carpenter on Warwickshire, Saul on Gloucestershire, Wright on Derbyshire) rebuilt the picture shire by shire. One structural fact drives it: power was personal, not institutional — no police, no bureaucracy at the village, royal courts that could not enforce their own judgments — so protection and justice were private goods, produced by networks of lordship. From that the whole economy follows: a market in good lordship (protection, offices, and backing at law, traded for service, counsel, and presence); livery as the advertisement of whose man would come if you were crossed — legislated against from 1390 precisely because it was load-bearing; arbitration and the loveday settling most disputes under the implicit weight of both parties' followings; and worship — standing — as the network's credit rating. The texture survives best in the Paston letters: Margaret Paston sending for crossbows to hold a manor house, the Duke of Norfolk besieging Caister (1469) over an inheritance, a rising family's frantic shopping for a lord great enough to protect it. Nibley Green (1470) — two retinues settling a Berkeley inheritance by pitched battle — and Sir Thomas Malory, the chivalric author indicted as ambusher and extortioner, mark the operating reality against the ideal.

In the module's terms the era is a dial-state, not a new system: assessments ancient, the margin universal, and the obligation migrated from the enfeoffed population to the retained one — the Muster section prices the retained man, and the era table dates the slide. The comparative literature reads the same machine wherever the state runs thin — private protection generally (Gambetta's study of the Sicilian mafia is the nearly exact parallel), the political machine, the big-man polity (Sahlins) — with one honest break: the medieval bond was dressed in homage, law, and religion, and was thought legitimate. The engine — weak state → private protection → power measured in men → wealth that must become followers to be safe — is the same.

Canon's two rules — the charter and the assessment, in print

This module built charter against muster from English evidence and its own doctrine: a paper obligation fixed at a grant, and a countryside that grows away from it. Offices

The office is the other way to set a man over land. RAW prices it already: a ruler may appoint magistrates (RR p.342) — captain of the guard, chaplain, munerator, steward — each overseeing one line of the domain's expenses and keeping 12.5% of the line he oversees as salary. The salary is carved out of the line itself (the ruler pays nothing extra and the troops are not reduced), it counts as the magistrate's own domain income for campaign XP, and RAW wants him sworn: magistrates are "usually fellow adventurers, followers, or henchmen," and "non-henchman magistrates might be susceptible to corruption or treachery." The sheriff and the constable of a royal castle are this machinery set over a district rather than the ruler's whole domain, overseeing two lines: the crown's tribute gathered through the castle, and the garrison it pays for. Command rides with the second line — the captain-of-the-guard delegations are inspecting troops, overseeing training, training troops, and waging war (RR p.352–3).15

The fisc, the corruption dial, and the office as anti-fee — the full doctrine

This is doctrine, declared: RAW's four magistrates oversee expense lines only, and none collects revenue — we add the fisc as a fifth oversight for district officers. RAW rewards the extension twice. Cash tribute decays with vassal count (Tribute Inefficiency, RR p.347): a third to half of every payment is lost to "crime, corruption, or waste" — and a sworn collector on a lawful 12.5% is exactly the cure the period applied. And the office is the anti-fee: paid in coin not land, revocable not heritable, loyal at 0 + the crown's CHA — the dubbed knight holds the same base 0, but his bond rides a heritable fee, where the office can be resumed with a word (only the undubbed and the defaulted sit at −2) — which is why crowns ran castles by commission while seating knights on fees. The opposite pole — the officer who keeps everything above a fixed render, as the early English sheriffs farmed their shires — is the Judges Journal's landowner/governor split (JJ p.405–6); this section prices the reformed, salaried custodian instead. And where the office is not held by a sworn man, RAW's warning gets numbers: the appointee skims a share of the spread — the gap between his lawful fee and the district's inefficiency band — sliding the crown's take from 87.5% back down toward the band itself. The two RAW tables are the endpoints; loyalty is the dial between them.

The dial at a glance — this district, the whole loyalty ladder

A vocabulary note first: the dial runs on the officer's loyalty — the man's stat (base −2 unsworn, −4 beyond the range of trade, RR p.347; "hostile" is the Hostility result of the Henchman Loyalty table) — not morale, which in ACKS II belongs to the district's population (base morale score, RR p.347–8). We read the base loyalty score itself as the dial position — RAW uses it as a roll modifier; the dial treats the −6…+2 axis as a scale of how bought the man is. Read the endpoints: the sworn man costs the lawful 12.5% (RR p.342); the hostile man delivers the crown exactly the inefficiency band (RR p.347) — RAW resumes when governance fails. Loyalty prices everything between. And because the salary is the officer's income for XP by RAW (p.342/423) — and the dial rules the skim the same way, its own extension — corruption levels the man: the unsworn officer grows himself out of the crown's own revenue stream — a self-funded villain, which is how a Sheriff of Nottingham happens. The gap between any row and the sworn man's row is what the bond is worth: closing it is what fosterage and household office-holding are for. The ladder is generated by one rule — an unsworn officer's skim share is (2 − loyalty) ÷ 8, clamped 0–1: at +2 good lordship collects like the sworn man, and by −6 the band has returned without waiting for a Hostility roll. And mind the render rate: the spread scales with the take, so the unsworn officer who exacts 2 gp/family where custom says 1 doubles fee and skim alike — raising the taxes can raise the man a level. Nottingham squeezed the shire for a reason.

The office in history — how a commission becomes a dynasty

The fee is heritable by design; the office is revocable by design. Nearly the whole medieval history of office is the second trying to become the first — and the dial on this page is that story compressed into one loyalty setting.

Start where the drift is clearest. A Carolingian comes was a royal officer: he held an honor — the office together with the lands that paid for it — at the king’s pleasure, and could in principle be moved or removed. In practice a man who commanded his district’s fighting men, in a century of raids and weak kings, was not easily dislodged; and a king who needed the district defended had every reason to leave a competent son in his father’s place. The Capitulary of Quierzy (877) is the marker usually reached for17. Within a few generations the countship is patrimony, and the office has become a fee. That is this tab’s warning case, not its model: everything below is about the technologies that hold the drift off.

England ran the same institution with the brake on. The sheriff — vicecomes — answered at the Exchequer for the firma comitatus, the county farm: a fixed annual sum for the shire’s revenues, the surplus his to keep. That is exactly the fee-farm pole of the dial (JJ p.405–6): the crown takes a known figure, and hands the risk, the effort and the squeeze to the man on the ground. It is cheap, it is administratively simple, and it is corrupting by construction — the officer’s income is the gap between what he collects and what he renders. Sheriffs grew rich enough that Henry II swept them out wholesale in the Inquest of Sheriffs (1170); later the counties were allowed to farm themselves and cut the middleman out of his own margin.

What kept an office an office was never the officer’s virtue. It was audit and term: the Exchequer’s twice-yearly account, the general eyre and the itinerant justices, the Provisions of Oxford (1258) demanding sheriffs drawn from the county and changed yearly, the elected hundred-constables of Winchester (1285). Rotation and accounting are the counter-technology to heredity, and both are expensive. Where a crown could not pay for them it farmed instead — and where it farmed long enough, it lost the office. The end of that road is venality: the office sold outright as property, and in France the paulette of 1604 letting a holder make his office heritable for an annual premium. At that point the crown has sold its own administration, and the journey from commission to estate is complete.

17 · Quierzy, 877 — a marker, not a moment DEBATED Charles the Bald, leaving for Italy, provided that if a count died in his absence the son might hold his father’s honor pending the king’s decision. It is routinely quoted as the birth of hereditary office, and it is not that: modern scholarship reads it as acknowledging a practice already running, not creating a right. Contested — cite it as a signpost on a slope, not a statute that made fiefs heritable. The direction of travel is what matters here, and on that the record is not divided.

The realm

Three ways to hold land, and a realm is the choice between them: keep it in demesne and take coin, grant it to a vassal and take tribute, grant it as a fee and take service. The tables below are yours to type into — the honour is a design, not a roll.

Vassals — land that renders tribute · RAW's own tier

A vassal rules his own domain, pays his own garrison, and renders tribute in coin — 18 gp × families0.6 (RR p.344). Grant him families and his grade follows; RAW's Titles of Nobility table names him. What he does inside his realm is his business — run this page again on his families to find out. And a vassal may hold vassals of his own: press ↓ to seat one under another, and each lord is banded only on the men directly under him16 — which is why the pyramid pays.
